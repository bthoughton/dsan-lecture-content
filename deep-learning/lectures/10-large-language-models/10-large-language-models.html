Today

Last week built the 2017 transformer. Today is one question: what had to change to get from that to a model you can hold a conversation with?

  • It got bigger, in a specific ratio that the field got wrong at first.
  • Four parts were replaced: how position is encoded, how it is normalized, how attention is stored, and what the MLP does.
  • It was trained differently: one objective, a very large pile of text, and a set of tricks that exist only to keep the run from diverging.
  • It was aligned afterwards, which is a separate training run and is most of what makes a chat model feel like one.

Then inference, which is where the money goes, and emergence, which is the part you have all heard about and where most of what gets said is loose.

What an LLM is

A large language model is a decoder-only transformer trained on a very large corpus to predict the next token, and nothing in its architecture is new.

Every part was on last week’s slides. What changed is scale, four component swaps, and two extra training stages after the pretraining.

That is worth being blunt about, because the marketing is not. There is no new mathematical idea between Attention Is All You Need and the model you used this morning. There is a great deal of engineering.

It got bigger

Five and a half years of scaling

From 65 million parameters to 1.7 trillion, about 26,154 times in under six years, or roughly 5 times a year. A straight line on a log axis is exponential growth.

Published figures, not measured here, and GPT-4’s is a reported estimate rather than a disclosure. Notice that the line stops being the whole story around 2022, which is the next slide.

Scaling laws

A scaling law is an empirical relationship between how much you spend and how well the model does. Kaplan et al., 2020, found that test loss falls as a power law in parameters, data and compute, with no sign of a floor.

That paper’s practical reading was that parameters mattered most, and the field acted on it: GPT-3 was 175 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens.

Hoffmann et al., 2022, redid the experiment over a wider range and found something different. For a fixed compute budget, parameters and training tokens should grow together, at roughly

D \approx 20\,N, \qquad \text{training FLOPs} \approx 6\,N\,D

for N parameters and D training tokens.

Most of the famous models were undertrained

GPT-3 saw 1.7 tokens per parameter where the compute-optimal ratio is about 20. The same compute spent on the line would have trained a 51 billion parameter model on far more data, and that model would have been better and cheaper to serve.

Chinchilla itself was the demonstration: 70 billion parameters, 1.4 trillion tokens, and it beat the 280 billion parameter Gopher trained on the same budget.

Why nobody trains compute-optimal any more

Chinchilla optimises training cost. Nobody trains a model once and stops; they serve it to millions of people, and serving cost scales with parameters, not with how many tokens it was trained on.

So the modern move is to go deliberately past the optimum: take a smaller model and train it far longer than Chinchilla would say. LLaMA-3-70B saw about 214 tokens per parameter, ten times the compute-optimal ratio.

The ratio you want depends on what you are paying for. Compute-optimal if you train once and throw it away; heavily overtrained if you have to serve it.

Four parts got replaced

Absolute position does not survive a long context

Week 9 added a positional vector to each embedding, either sinusoidal or learned. Both break as context grows.

  • A learned table has a fixed number of rows. Position 2,049 of a 2,048 row table does not exist, so you cannot run on a longer sequence at all.
  • Sinusoidal encoding is defined everywhere, but the model has only ever seen the pattern for positions it trained on, and it does not reliably generalize past them.
  • Both encode where a token is, when attention almost always wants to know how far apart two tokens are.

The fix is to stop adding position to the embedding and start applying it to the query and the key instead.

Rotary position embedding

RoPE rotates each pair of dimensions in the query and key by an angle proportional to the position. Rotating two vectors by the same amount does not change the angle between them, so a dot product of two rotated vectors depends on their positions only through the difference.

The right panel is the measurement. Holding the gap fixed and sliding the pair along the sequence, the rotary score changes by 5e-6, which is floating point noise. The absolute encoding changes by 21.4 over the same slide. RoPE is in LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen and most of what is current.

RoPE in code

import torch

torch.manual_seed(6600)
d, base = 64, 10_000.0


def rope(x, pos):
    """Rotate each pair of dimensions by an angle proportional to position."""
    half = d // 2
    inv = base ** (-torch.arange(half, dtype=torch.float32) * 2 / d)
    ang = pos[:, None] * inv[None, :]
    x1, x2 = x[..., :half], x[..., half:]
    return torch.cat([x1 * ang.cos() - x2 * ang.sin(),
                      x1 * ang.sin() + x2 * ang.cos()], dim=-1)


q, k = torch.randn(d), torch.randn(d)
print("the score depends only on the gap, not the position:")
for a, b in [(0, 3), (10, 13), (40, 43), (100, 103)]:
    qa = rope(q[None, :], torch.tensor([float(a)]))
    kb = rope(k[None, :], torch.tensor([float(b)]))
    print(f"  positions {a:>3} and {b:>3}  (gap 3)   score {float(qa @ kb.T):+.6f}")

print()
print("a different gap gives a different score, as it should:")
for a, b in [(0, 7), (40, 47)]:
    qa = rope(q[None, :], torch.tensor([float(a)]))
    kb = rope(k[None, :], torch.tensor([float(b)]))
    print(f"  positions {a:>3} and {b:>3}  (gap 7)   score {float(qa @ kb.T):+.6f}")

Normalization: LayerNorm to RMSNorm

Week 9 showed why sequence models normalize over features. RMSNorm keeps that and drops the mean:

\text{LayerNorm}(x) = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\,g + b, \qquad \text{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d}\sum_i x_i^2}}\,g

No mean subtraction and no bias, so it is one pass over the features rather than two. It performs about the same and is measurably cheaper, which at this scale is reason enough.

The other change is where it goes. The original paper normalized after the residual add; every large model now normalizes before the sublayer (norm_first=True), because post-norm networks of 50 or more blocks are unstable to train.

Attention: the KV cache, and then shrinking it

Generation is one token per forward pass, and every new token attends to every previous one. Recomputing the keys and values of the whole prefix each step makes generating T tokens cost O(T^2).

The KV cache stores the projected keys and values and appends one column per step, so each step is O(1) of projection plus one attention against the store. It is not an optimization you can skip; it is how generation is done.

The cache then becomes the problem. It is per sequence, per layer, and grows with the conversation.

What the cache costs

A 7 billion parameter model at a 32,000 token context needs about 17 GB of cache for one sequence with ordinary multi-head attention. Serving many users at once, that, not the weights, is what fills the GPU.

Grouped query attention is the fix: keep all the query heads, share keys and values across groups of them. Eight key-value heads instead of 32 brings that 32k cache to 4.3 GB. The right panel measures why the cache exists at all: at 1,024 tokens of context, producing one more token costs 10 times less with the cache than without.

The MLP: GELU to SwiGLU

Week 9’s feed forward network was two linear layers with a nonlinearity, widening to 4d and back. Modern models use a gated variant:

\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \big(\text{Swish}(xW_1) \odot xW_3\big)\,W_2

Three matrices instead of two. One branch computes values and the other computes a gate that scales them elementwise, which is the same idea as an LSTM’s gates applied per position instead of per timestep.

Because there are three matrices, the hidden width is usually cut to about \tfrac{8}{3}d to keep the parameter count the same. The FFN is about two thirds of a transformer’s parameters, so this is not a small part of the model.

Where the four changes land

part 2017 now why
position added to the embedding RoPE, rotating Q and K relative, and extends past the trained length
normalization LayerNorm, after the residual RMSNorm, before the sublayer cheaper, and stable at depth
attention recompute each step KV cache, then grouped query generation would be quadratic; the cache then has to shrink
the MLP two matrices and GELU SwiGLU, three matrices better at equal parameter count

None of these change what a transformer is. Attention, multi-head, the residuals, the causal mask and the block structure are all exactly last week’s.

How it is trained

One objective

Causal language modelling: predict the next token, for every position at once.

\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\!\left(x_t \mid x_{<t}\right)

Which is cross entropy with the next token as the label, and the causal mask from last week is what makes computing all T terms in one pass legitimate.

That is the whole pretraining objective. No labels, no human input, nothing task-specific. The text is its own supervision, which is why it scales: the limit is how much text you can find, not how much you can afford to annotate.

Training data

Modern pretraining corpora are measured in trillions of tokens, assembled from web crawl, code, books, and reference text. The work is almost entirely in what you remove:

  • deduplication, exact and near. Duplicated text gets memorized rather than learned, and it inflates your benchmark scores.
  • quality filtering, usually a classifier trained to recognise text that looks like a curated source.
  • decontamination, removing anything that appears in the evaluation sets. This one is routinely done badly, which is why benchmark numbers should be read with suspicion.

Data work is most of the job and almost none of the papers. The architecture has barely moved; the corpora have moved enormously.

Tokenization at this scale

Week 8’s subword tokenizer, with the vocabulary size now a real engineering decision. Typical modern vocabularies run 32,000 to 256,000.

A larger vocabulary means shorter sequences, which is quadratically cheaper in attention, and a bigger embedding table and output layer, which is linearly more parameters and more softmax work. Somewhere in the middle is the right answer, and it depends on the languages you care about.

The costs show up in odd places. A tokenizer fitted mostly to English spends several tokens per word on other languages, so the same sentence costs more to process and more to buy. Numbers and code are similarly sensitive to how digits and whitespace were split.

Keeping a long run alive

A training run of this size is one job for weeks on thousands of accelerators, and the practical content of a technical report is mostly about not losing it.

  • Learning rate: a linear warmup over the first fraction of a percent of steps, then a cosine decay. Warmup exists because the first updates at full learning rate reliably diverge.
  • Gradient clipping by global norm, usually at 1.0.
  • bf16 rather than fp16: the same 16 bits, more exponent and less mantissa, which matters because the failure mode at this scale is overflow.
  • Checkpoint often, because hardware fails and you will be restarting from a checkpoint, not from zero.

How the work is split

One model no longer fits on one device, so the work is cut up. You should recognize the names; you will not be asked to implement any of them.

kind what is split cost
data parallel the batch, model replicated memory, every replica holds everything
tensor parallel individual matrices, across devices communication inside every layer
pipeline parallel whole layers, across devices devices idle waiting for each other
ZeRO / FSDP the optimizer state, gradients and parameters communication, in exchange for a much smaller footprint

The thing worth taking away: optimizer state is the memory problem, not weights. Adam keeps two moments per parameter, so training a model costs several times what storing it does.

Making it useful

A pretrained model is not an assistant

A model trained only to predict the next token does exactly that. Ask one a question and a plausible continuation is another question, because that is what documents full of questions look like.

Nothing is wrong with the model. It was never trained to be helpful, and helpfulness is not a property of text statistics.

Two further stages close that gap, and together they are what separates a base model from a chat model.

Supervised fine-tuning

SFT is ordinary supervised training on demonstrations: prompt and response pairs written or curated by people, with the loss taken on the response only.

The objective is unchanged from pretraining; only the data changed. That is why it is cheap, typically thousands to hundreds of thousands of examples rather than trillions of tokens.

SFT is what installs the shape of a conversation: that a question gets an answer, that instructions get followed, that there is a turn structure at all. It does not install judgement about which of two decent answers is better, because a demonstration gives one answer and no comparison.

Learning from preferences

The next stage uses comparisons instead of demonstrations: given one prompt and two responses, a human says which is better. Comparing is far easier than writing, so this data is cheaper to collect and more consistent.

RLHF turns those comparisons into a reward model that scores responses, then optimizes the language model against that score with reinforcement learning, usually PPO, with a penalty for drifting too far from the SFT model.

DPO skips the reward model. The same preference data is turned directly into a loss on the language model, which is simpler, has fewer moving parts and performs comparably. Much of the field moved to it or a variant.

You should be able to say what each is for. You will not be asked to derive either objective.

What alignment does and does not do

Does. Make it follow instructions, adopt a consistent format and register, refuse some categories of request, and prefer answers people rate highly.

Does not. Make it know anything it did not learn in pretraining, or make it accurate. Those are pretraining properties, and no amount of preference data adds a fact that was never in the corpus.

And the failure mode is specific. Optimizing against a learned reward model rewards answers that score well, which is not the same as answers that are good. Confident, fluent, agreeable and wrong scores well. That is most of what people mean by an LLM being sycophantic, and it is a predictable consequence of the training objective rather than a mystery.

Inference

How the next token is chosen

A real distribution out of GPT-2. The top token is Paris, and it is correct, but it has only 0.07 of the probability mass.

Temperature divides the logits before the softmax: below 1 sharpens toward the top token, above 1 flattens. Top-p keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probability sums to p and renormalizes, so the number kept adapts to how confident the model is: 52 tokens at p = 0.5 here, 1,018 at p = 0.9, out of 50,257.

Greedy decoding, always taking the top token, is temperature 0. It is right for extraction and wrong for anything that should read naturally.

Sampling in code

import torch

torch.manual_seed(6600)
# a stand-in for one real next-token distribution: a few likely tokens and a
# long tail, which is the shape the GPT-2 figure showed
logits = torch.cat([torch.tensor([6.0, 5.4, 5.1, 4.4]), torch.randn(996) - 1])


def sample(logits, temperature=1.0, top_p=1.0):
    p = (logits / temperature).softmax(-1)
    order = p.argsort(descending=True)
    keep = (p[order].cumsum(0) - p[order]) < top_p      # always keeps the first
    p = torch.zeros_like(p).scatter_(0, order[keep], p[order][keep])
    return p / p.sum()


for temp in (0.2, 1.0, 1.6):
    p = sample(logits, temperature=temp)
    print(f"  temperature {temp:<4} top token {p.max():.3f}   "
          f"tokens above 1%: {(p > 0.01).sum():>3}")
print()
for tp in (0.5, 0.9, 1.0):
    p = sample(logits, top_p=tp)
    print(f"  top-p {tp:<5} keeps {(p > 0).sum():>4} of {len(logits)} tokens")

  temperature 1.0  top token 0.268   tokens above 1%:   5
  temperature 1.6  top token 0.055   tokens above 1%:   4

  top-p 0.9   keeps  370 of 1000 tokens
  top-p 1.0   keeps 1000 of 1000 tokens

Why serving is expensive

Conflating these two is the most common mistake in capacity planning.

Decode speed tracks memory bandwidth, not FLOPs. A faster GPU with the same memory bandwidth generates at nearly the same rate.

Making it cheaper

technique what it does what it costs
quantization store weights in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16 a little quality, usually surprisingly little
grouped query attention shrink the KV cache a little quality
continuous batching start new requests as others finish nothing, it is scheduling
paged attention page the cache like virtual memory nothing, it is bookkeeping
speculative decoding a small model drafts, the big one checks in parallel nothing, output is identical

Speculative decoding is the elegant one. A draft model proposes several tokens, the real model verifies them in a single forward pass, and any prefix it agrees with is kept. The output distribution is provably unchanged.

Prompting, in one slide

Everything the model will use is in its context window, so prompting is just deciding what goes there. Chain of thought is asking for the reasoning before the answer, which helps because each generated token is another forward pass: writing the steps out gives the model more computation to spend.

Retrieval does the same thing with facts rather than reasoning: find the relevant text, put it in the context, and the model reads it rather than recalling it. Nothing here changes the weights.

Emergent capabilities

An emergent capability is one that is near chance at smaller scales and then rises sharply past some size, rather than improving smoothly. Arithmetic, multi-step reasoning and following an unfamiliar instruction format are the usual examples.

The honest caveat matters as much as the claim. Several of these curves look like step functions because the metric is a step function: exact-match scoring gives no credit for being nearly right, so smooth underlying improvement shows up as a sudden jump. Change to a graded metric and some emergence disappears.

Some of it survives that correction and some does not. Treat a claimed emergent ability as a claim about the metric until you have checked.

Evaluating these is genuinely hard

Benchmarks are cheap to game and easy to contaminate, and the two interact: if a test set was on the web before the crawl, a high score may be memorization.

What tends to survive contact with reality: held-out perplexity on text you collected after the model shipped, task suites you built yourself from your own data, and human comparison, which is expensive and still the thing everyone falls back on.

The practical version for your project: do not trust a published score on a public benchmark as evidence that a model will work on your problem. Build a small evaluation set of your own, by hand if necessary, and use that.

Wrap-up

Wrap-up

  • An LLM is a decoder-only transformer. Nothing in the architecture is new since 2017.
  • Scaling laws say parameters and tokens should grow together, about 20 tokens per parameter, and most famous models were well off that line.
  • Modern serving cost pushes past compute-optimal on purpose.
  • Four parts were replaced: RoPE, RMSNorm before the sublayer, the KV cache plus grouped query attention, and SwiGLU.
  • Pretraining is one objective, next-token prediction, on a corpus whose construction is most of the work.
  • SFT installs the format; preference optimization installs the judgement. Neither adds knowledge.
  • Generation is memory-bound, which is why the KV cache governs what you can serve.
  • Treat emergence and benchmark scores as claims about a metric until checked.
