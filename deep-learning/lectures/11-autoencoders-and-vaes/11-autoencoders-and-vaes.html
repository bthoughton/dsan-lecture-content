Three content meetings left, and they are the generative ones. Autoencoders earn their place twice over. On their own they do real work, mostly anomaly detection, which is the half of today you could ship next week. And the compressor you see today is the specific piece that makes image diffusion affordable, which is next week. That is not a general law: the language models from Week 10 generate text with no compressor anywhere in them.

What an autoencoder is

An autoencoder is a network trained to reproduce its own input through a bottleneck too small to pass everything.

The encoder compresses the input to a code, the decoder rebuilds the input from that code, and the loss is the difference between what went in and what came out. No labels anywhere. The bottleneck is the whole design: without it the network can learn the identity function and learn nothing.

How small should the bottleneck be

Under-complete means the code is smaller than the input, which forces the network to throw something away and keep what matters. Over-complete means it is as large or larger, and then nothing stops it copying the input straight through. If you want an over-complete code to be useful you have to constrain it another way, with noise or with a penalty on the activations.

Autoencoders and PCA

An autoencoder with linear layers and a squared-error loss spans the same subspace PCA finds.

  • Add nonlinear activations and it bends, which PCA cannot

This is the sentence that places autoencoders for anyone who has used PCA: it is nonlinear dimensionality reduction. The handwritten note is the caveat. The linear autoencoder finds the same subspace but not the same basis, so the axes are not ordered by variance and are not the principal components.

What people actually use them for

Segmentation is on that list for its shape, not its training. A U-Net is an encoder-decoder, but it learns from labeled masks, so it is not an autoencoder.

The ones that pay are dimensionality reduction, denoising, and anomaly detection, and we will take those in turn.

Segmentation deserves the caveat because the list is misleading without it. Nobody trains an autoencoder to segment. What happened is that the encoder-decoder shape was borrowed, which is the U-Net from Week 7. The live connection today is the other direction: a masked autoencoder reconstructs hidden patches with no labels at all, and that pretrained encoder is then fine-tuned for segmentation or detection. Reconstruction is the pretraining task, never the segmentation task itself.

Compression

Send the code instead of the image, and rebuild it at the other end. This is not how JPEG works and it is not usually how you should compress a photograph, because a general codec is better and does not need training. It is a useful picture because it makes clear that the code has to carry everything the decoder will need.

Denoising

A denoising autoencoder is trained with corrupted input and the clean original as the target.

It cannot copy its input, because its input is wrong. The only way to lower the loss is to learn what the data normally looks like. Hold on to this one. Next week’s diffusion model is this idea taken to its limit: destroy the data in a way you control, then train a network to undo it.

Convolutional autoencoders

For images, replace the dense layers with convolutions down and transposed convolutions back up.

Same idea, better inductive bias. A dense layer on a 256 by 256 image has billions of parameters and no notion that nearby pixels are related. The convolutional version is the one that gets used, and it is the shape of the U-Net from Week 7 and the denoiser you will meet next week.

Inpainting

Mask part of the image, ask the network to produce the whole thing, and score it against the original. It has to infer the missing region from the context, which is a harder version of denoising. This is the same task Photoshop’s content-aware fill does, and it is also what a diffusion model does when you use it for inpainting next week.

Anomaly detection

Train on normal data only. The model gets good at rebuilding the kind of thing it saw and stays bad at everything else, so reconstruction error becomes a score for “unlike the training data”. This is the most shippable thing in today’s lecture, and the reason is that it needs no labels for the anomalies, which in a real anomaly problem you do not have.

Where to put the threshold

The errors on normal data form a distribution, and the cut goes out in its tail. Picking the cut is picking your false-alarm rate, and that is a business decision rather than a modeling one: how many good transactions are you willing to stop in order to catch one bad one. Note the threshold is set without ever looking at an anomaly.

Does it actually work

A 16-unit bottleneck trained on MNIST for 20 epochs, then shown Fashion-MNIST. 95% of the clothes land past the threshold, for 1% false alarms.

The threshold is the 99th percentile of the training errors. It never sees a single anomaly, which is the point: at deployment you do not have them either. Fraud, machine failure, bad sensor readings, and manufacturing defects are all this shape.

What generation would require

Draw a code from a distribution you can sample, decode it, get something new.

Notice what this picture assumes. There is a distribution on the left you can draw from, and every draw decodes to something plausible. An autoencoder gives you a decoder, so it looks like it should work. The next three slides are why it does not.

The latent space has no required shape

The training objective says rebuild the input. It says nothing about where the codes go. Any of those three layouts gets the same reconstruction loss, so the encoder is free to pick whichever one is convenient, and it will usually leave large empty regions between the clusters.

So you cannot sample from it

Decoding a point the encoder never produced usually gives nonsense.

Pick a point between the cat cluster and the dog cluster and decode it. Nothing in training required that point to mean anything, and you get the hybrid. The problem is not that the decoder is weak. It is that nobody ever asked the space to be continuous.

A VAE encodes a region

A variational autoencoder’s encoder outputs a mean and a spread instead of a single point, and a fresh code is drawn from that region on every pass.

Because the code moves every pass, nearby codes have to decode to similar things. That is what fills in the gaps. The decoder is no longer memorizing a lookup from a handful of points; it has to make the whole neighborhood work.

The loss function

Two terms on a balance. Reconstruction is the autoencoder loss you already know, and it pulls codes apart so inputs stay distinguishable. The KL term measures how far the encoder’s output distribution sits from a standard normal and pulls everything back toward it.

Read the second term carefully, because it names a specific target distribution. It is not asking for a tidy latent space in the abstract. It is asking for one particular distribution, the one you will later draw from. That is what makes sampling possible, and the next slide is what it buys.

What the KL term buys

  • Continuity. The points between two codes decode to something sensible
  • A known place to draw from. The codes pile up on N(0, I)

Two separate jobs, and you need both. Continuity is the local one, and it is what the handwritten notes on the left panel are about: a point that decodes to nothing, and two nearby points that decode to unrelated things.

The second is global and is easy to skip past. Pulling the codes onto a standard normal tells you where the codes live. A latent space could be perfectly continuous and still be useless to sample, if its codes sat in a blob centred at four hundred with a spread of fifty, because you would have no idea what distribution to draw from. The KL term fixes the address, not just the smoothness.

Run reconstruction alone and you get the left panel. Run KL alone and everything collapses onto the prior and the decoder learns nothing.

The reparameterization trick

Drawing a sample is not a differentiable operation, so a gradient cannot pass through it. Writing the draw as mu plus sigma times noise moves the randomness into an input, and the path from the loss back to mu and sigma is ordinary arithmetic again. This is a mechanism rather than a decision you will make, but it is a standard interview question.

The same bottleneck, both losses

Two units, 20 epochs, same data. The autoencoder spreads to 28; the VAE stays inside 5.1.

Look at the axes before the colors. The autoencoder has spokes and empty wedges, and no reason to prefer any particular scale. The VAE is a single blob around the origin, because that is what the KL term asked for. Now a random draw from a standard normal lands somewhere the decoder understands.

Walking the latent space

A grid of 324 points, none of which came from an image. Both halves of the KL term’s job are visible here, and it is worth separating them.

Continuity is why the squares between the digits are digits at all, and why 7 slides into 9 and 3 into 8 instead of going through noise.

The axis range is the other half. This grid runs from minus 2.2 to plus 2.2, and it is full, because the codes were pulled onto a standard normal and that square covers almost all of them. On the plain autoencoder from the last slide the codes ran out past 28, so the same square would have been nearly empty. Continuity tells you the space is smooth. The prior tells you where to look.

The axes can mean something

Move one latent coordinate and hold the rest still, and sometimes a single human-readable property changes: glasses, hair colour, pose. Nothing in the loss asked for that. It happens because the KL term pushes the coordinates to be independent of one another, and independent coordinates tend to line up with independent factors in the data.

How much structure to ask for

A weight on the KL term. Too small and the gaps come back. Too large and every input decodes to the same thing.

This is the beta-VAE, and beta is the only hyperparameter of the method worth knowing. The right-hand panel is the failure people hit in practice and misread as a bug: the model has satisfied the KL term perfectly by ignoring the input, and the reconstruction loss is the only thing objecting.

Why VAE images look soft

Squared error on pixels is minimized by averaging over everything the code could have meant.

The blur is the loss function, not a bug. Given an ambiguous code, the safest prediction under squared error is the mean of all the plausible images, and the mean of many faces is a smooth face. Next week is about two ways around this.

Wrap-up

  • An autoencoder compresses with no labels. The bottleneck is the design
  • Nonlinear PCA is the honest one-line description
  • Anomaly detection is the job you can ship: train on normal, threshold the error
  • A plain latent space has holes, so you cannot sample from it
  • A VAE encodes a region, so the gaps have to decode
  • The KL term does two jobs: continuity, and a known place to draw from
  • Beta sets how hard the space is pushed toward a normal
  • The blur is the squared-error loss, not a bug
